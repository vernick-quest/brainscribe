The assignment says *under 500 words*. Your kid writes 200, looks up, and says they're done.

And they're right. They complied. Nothing about the instruction they were given says otherwise.

That's the whole problem in one sentence. **A maximum is a boundary, not a goal.** A kid who lands at 200 has no way to know they just handed in half an assignment.

## Why the maximum is the only number on the page

Teachers state maximums for a good reason: it protects them from the 2,000-word submission. What they rarely state is a minimum — and that's also for a good reason. Putting a floor on an assignment feels like inviting the bare minimum. Write "at least 300 words" and you'll get 304 of them, every time.

So the number that appears is a ceiling, and the real expectation goes unstated. The student is left to infer it.

Which is fine for the kid who reads the room easily. It is not fine for the kid this whole product exists for. **Inferring an unstated expectation is precisely what struggling writers are worst at** — the same executive-function gap that makes starting hard, makes "how much is enough?" unanswerable. They don't underperform the expectation. They never see it.

And when a draft comes in far under the ceiling, it's usually not that the writing is short. Something got left out:

- A claim that never got its evidence.
- An example that got named but not explained.
- A "because" that stops one step too early.
- The obvious objection, skipped.

The length isn't the missing thing. It's where the missing thing shows up.

## We had two options and both were wrong

Once we could see the gap, the tempting fix was to enforce a target — hold the student to a range and don't let them finish below it.

That's a bad idea for reasons that took us about a day to admit. It punishes a kid whose work is genuinely complete at 320 words. It turns "you're done" into an argument with a machine. And the only reliable way for a student to satisfy a length gate is to pad — restating the thesis, stacking adjectives, a sentence that says the last sentence again. Padding is worse writing. Building a feature whose most likely outcome is worse writing is not a feature.

The other option was to leave it alone. But leaving it alone means shrugging at the exact gap we just described, for the exact students least equipped to close it.

So: nudge, not gate.

## What that actually sounds like

When a draft is sitting well under the room the assignment allows, the coach mentions it once — in words, the way a person would:

> *"There's room up to 500 here — anything you wanted to say that got left out? No pressure if it feels complete."*

And then it signs off either way. If the student says it's done, it's done. No blocking, no scolding, no progress bar shaming them toward a number, no second ask.

That posture is the part I'd point at. Most tools that know a target use it to nag — because nagging is easy to build and it demos well. Deciding that the student gets to overrule the nudge, every time, is the actual design decision. The arithmetic underneath it is the boring part.

Two things that follow from it, which matter more than they sound:

**The student never sees a percentage, and neither should you.** The coach talks in words, because words are what the assignment is in. Any formula sitting behind that is implementation detail, and if we ever put it in front of a kid we've made the thing we were trying to fix — an abstract standard they have to decode — slightly worse.

**No stated limit means no target.** Plenty of assignments don't give a number at all. Those get no range and no mention of length, because we'd be inventing an expectation the teacher never set. Better to say nothing than to make one up.

## It still can't write a word

Worth being explicit, since "the coach wants more words" could be read the wrong way: it has no words of its own to give. It never has. When it nudges, the only move available to it is to ask a question and see what the student says back. If they've got nothing more to say, the draft stays 320 words.

Which is why "nudge" is the honest verb. It can prompt more thinking. It cannot manufacture more prose, and the coaching rules forbid it from trying.

## The thing we're deliberately not doing

There's a well-documented finding in writing-assessment research that longer work tends to receive higher marks — [Fleckenstein and colleagues (2020)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.562462/full) found text length accounted for a substantial share of the variance in raters' quality scores *beyond* students' actual measured proficiency, and described it as partly a genuine signal and partly a judgment bias.

We're aware of that. We're not coaching to it. Training a kid to write longer because graders reward length is teaching them to game a scoring artifact, and it's the opposite of everything else this product does. The nudge exists because a short draft is often a *symptom* of an underdeveloped idea — not because more words score better.

## Three questions that work at the kitchen table

You don't need the feature to do this. Pick a paragraph and ask:

1. **"You said this — how do you know?"** The answer is usually the missing evidence, already in their head.
2. **"What would someone who disagrees say?"** Kids often have a real answer and no idea they were allowed to include it.
3. **"Which part would a reader not believe yet?"** This one is remarkable. Kids almost always know exactly which paragraph is thin.

None of these is *make it longer*. They're all *say more of what you already think* — and the length follows, or it doesn't, and either answer is fine.
