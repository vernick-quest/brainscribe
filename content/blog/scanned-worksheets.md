A lot of writing homework still arrives on paper. A book report template, a reading response, a graphic organiser with six boxes and a dotted line under each one. So we added photo uploads: take a picture of the sheet, and the coach reads the assignment off it.

That part worked well. It reads a two-column table, handwriting at an angle, a rating scale with one of the stars circled.

Then a parent tried it with a sheet his son had **already partly filled in** — and we found the flaw.

## We were reading the form and throwing away the writing

The upload came back with every field intact and every answer gone:

```
- Book Title: [student fills in]
- Author's Name: [student fills in]
- I chose this book because... [student writes response]
```

It hadn't failed to see his answers. It had seen them, decided they weren't part of *the assignment*, and helpfully handed back the blank template.

Which, technically, is what we'd asked for. The instruction said *extract the writing assignment* — and a student's own answers aren't the assignment. It did exactly as it was told, and the result was a kid being asked to redo work he'd already done.

The first message made it worse. Our opener asked *"Have you written anything so far?"* — of a page where he plainly had. He had to type back: *"yes it should be in the upload."*

That's a small moment, and it's the kind that costs you a student. Nothing tanks trust faster than being asked about something you can already see.

## The real question wasn't technical

Once the coach could see the existing answers, we had to decide what to do with them. There were two obvious options, and both were wrong.

**Take it all as done.** Read the answers in, mark every box complete, move on. Respectful of their time — and useless. Most students who bring in a half-finished worksheet are here *because* some of it is thin. A one-line answer to "what did this book make you think about?" is exactly the box that needs a second pass. Rubber-stamping it wastes the visit.

**Start over.** Ignore what's there, work through every question from scratch. Thorough — and insulting. They did that work. Making them do it again reads as "none of that counted," and it's the fastest way to have a kid close the tab.

The parent who found this put it plainly: his son's book report *wasn't enough detail* — that's why he sent him to BrainScribe in the first place. But starting from zero felt like a step backwards.

So we did neither.

## What the coach does now

It reads the sheet back, locks in what's genuinely finished, and asks about the parts that could go further — one at a time, and it takes no for an answer.

Four boxes locked in without discussion, because a book title doesn't need coaching. One question asked about the box that was thin. And an explicit *you can keep it exactly as it is* — because it's the student's report, not ours.

That last answer is the whole point. It was already in his head. Nobody had asked for it.

## Why we won't just fill in the blanks

The obvious "feature" here would be a button that finishes the sheet. We won't build it, for the same reason we won't write the essay: the moment the coach supplies the words, the work stops being the student's.

So the rules are narrow. Their existing answers go in **exactly as written** — spelling and all — and the coach never tidies them on the way in. It can ask what made them think that. It cannot suggest what they might have meant. If an answer changes, it changes because the student said something new, out loud, in their own words.

Everything else stays the same: you can read the whole session afterwards, including which parts came in on the paper and which came out of the conversation.

## The honest version of this story

We shipped a feature, a parent used it on real homework, and it did something we hadn't thought about. He told us within the hour. It was fixed the same evening.

That's not a triumphant arc, but it's the actual one, and it's the reason we'd rather have a small number of families using this properly than a big number using it politely. If you try something and it does something daft, tell us. It's the fastest way this gets good.
