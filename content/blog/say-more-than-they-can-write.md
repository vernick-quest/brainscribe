Ask your kid what they thought of the book and you'll get three minutes of it — the character they didn't trust, the part that made them mad, the ending they'd have written differently. Ask them to put a paragraph of that on the page and you get: *The book was good. I liked the main character.*

Same kid. Same ideas. Ten minutes apart.

It's easy to read that as not trying. It's almost never that. There's a gap between what a kid can *say* and what they can *get down*, and for some kids that gap is enormous.

## Why the gap exists

Writing asks the brain to do two jobs at the same time. One is composing — deciding what you think and how to say it. The other is transcription — spelling the words, forming the letters or finding the keys, placing the punctuation. Both draw on the same limited pool of attention and working memory.

Virginia Berninger, professor emerita of educational psychology at the University of Washington, spent decades on federally funded research into how children develop as writers. She described transcription as a genuine constraint on composition, not a separate finishing step. When transcription is hard, it doesn't just slow the writing down — it crowds out the thinking. The good sentence your kid had in their head is gone by the time they've worked out how to spell the third word in it.

That's why the same idea comes out rich out loud and thin on paper. Talking has no transcription cost. Nothing to spell, nothing to punctuate, no keys to find. All the attention goes to the idea.

For a kid with [dysgraphia](/writing-help/dysgraphia), the transcription cost is high by definition. For a kid with ADHD, the working memory doing the juggling tires fast. For a twice-exceptional kid — big ideas, effortful output — the gap between the two is the widest and the most frustrating, because they can hear how much better the spoken version was.

## What helps tonight

You don't need a program to shrink the gap. A few things move the needle right away.

**Let them talk it out first, before anything is written.** Ask what they want to say and just listen. Not "let's outline" — a conversation. Most kids will say something better than they'd have written, and now it exists.

**Write down their words, not your words.** If you're capturing while they talk, resist the urge to improve it. The sentence has to still sound like them, or they'll disown the whole paragraph and you're back at the start.

**Separate the two jobs on purpose.** Get the ideas out first, in whatever rough form. Fix spelling and punctuation later, as its own pass. Trying to do both at once is the thing that jams.

**Let them dictate when the point is thinking.** For a first draft or a brainstorm, speaking is the faster road. Steve Graham, whose work at Arizona State has synthesized decades of writing research, has long pointed to dictation as a way for struggling writers to produce fuller, better compositions than they manage by hand. Handwriting and spelling still matter — they're just not the same lesson as *what do I actually want to say*.
