It usually starts quietly. There's an essay due, and your kid is at the table with it open. An hour later the page is still mostly blank, you ask how it's going, and something in your voice — or theirs — lands wrong. Now it's not about the essay anymore. It's a standoff. You're frustrated because nothing's happening; they're frustrated because you're hovering; and the assignment is somehow further away than when you started.

If that scene is familiar, you're not doing anything wrong. Writing has a way of becoming the flashpoint that math worksheets and reading logs don't. It helps to understand why — and then to have a few small moves ready that lower the temperature instead of raising it.

## Why writing becomes the fight

A math problem gives you somewhere to start. Writing gives you a blank page and a hundred decisions at once — what to say first, how to begin, whether the idea is any good. For a lot of kids, that pile of decisions is genuinely paralyzing, and the stall that follows looks, from across the room, exactly like not trying.

So the parent leans in. Reasonable — the deadline is real. But leaning in adds pressure, and pressure is the one thing that makes starting even harder. Now you're both pushing on a door that only opens the other way. The fight isn't really about effort. It's two people stuck on the same locked step, blaming each other for it.

## Name the stuck point, not the kid

"Why haven't you started?" sounds like an accusation, even when you don't mean it that way. "Getting started on these is the worst part, huh?" says the same thing without the blame — and it's true. When a kid hears that you get *why* it's hard, the standoff loses most of its charge. You're on the same side of the door again.

## Get them talking before they write

Here's a quiet truth about writing: talking is far easier than transcribing. The ideas are usually already in there — what's stuck is the getting-them-out. So don't ask for a sentence. Ask a question they can answer out loud. "What's the one thing you actually want the reader to get?" Let them ramble. What they say *is* the first draft; it just isn't on the page yet. Once a kid hears their own idea out loud, the blank page is a lot less blank.

## Shrink the next step until it's almost silly

"Write the introduction" is an assignment. "Just say the first thing out loud" is a step. When a kid is frozen, the job isn't to get them to finish — it's to get them unstuck, once. Make the next move so small it's almost silly to refuse. Momentum does the rest of the work that willpower can't.

## Step out of the referee chair

The hardest part of homework-battle nights is the role you get cast in: enforcer, timekeeper, the person who has to keep asking. It's a role that pits you against your kid on the one task where they most need to feel safe being stuck. You don't have to play it. Sometimes the most useful thing you can do is hand the "keep going" job to someone — or something — that isn't you, so you can go back to being on their side.

It won't end every homework night, but it can take you out of the referee chair — which is often where the fight was coming from in the first place.
